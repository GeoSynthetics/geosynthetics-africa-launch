Open reservoirs and ponds lose water to the sun, grow algae, collect dust and bird droppings and, on effluent dams, release odour. A floating cover fixes all of it, and a well-made one lasts: our FC60 covers carry a 30-year warranty (20 years full plus 10 years pro-rata). But a cover only performs if it stays tight. A loose cover ponds rainwater, flaps and tears in wind and is unsafe to walk on. The defined sump layout solves that with two simple parts: floats and ballast.

This guide covers what floating covers do for each type of reservoir, how a defined sump cover works, how we lay it out, and what to put in your specification.

What a defined sump cover is

A defined sump cover combines rows of floats with rows of sand-filled ballast tubes. Both are sealed inside geomembrane sleeves welded to the cover, so nothing is exposed to sun or water. The ballast pulls the cover down between the float rows and forms drainage channels, the sumps, across the surface. The same pull tensions the membrane, so the cover stays taut whatever the water level.

How the cover stays tight

Floats lift the cover on either side of each sump, and the ballast tube pulls the middle down. The two forces tension the membrane across the reservoir. As the water rises, the spare material that would otherwise wrinkle on the surface is drawn into the sumps, which simply get deeper. A tensioned cover is safer to walk on, easier to keep clean and far more secure in high wind.

Getting rainwater off the cover

  1. Collect. Rain runs off the taut cover, drains through the gaps between the segmented floats and collects in the sumps, away from the stored water.
  2. Pump. Scavenging pumps sit in the sumps at the sump intersections, held in a buoyant, membrane-encapsulated foam collar so they follow the water level.
  3. Discharge. When a pump switches on it empties the sump through a discharge hose that runs, with its power cable, along the sump and off the cover.
  4. Vent. Floating vents, a floating base under the membrane with a boot and vent riser fitted from above, let air and gas escape from under the cover.

Choosing a sump layout

There are several ways to lay out the sumps. Three patterns cover most reservoirs. We choose between them from the reservoir's shape and size, the side slopes, and where the pumps and discharge lines can sit.

Floats and ballast: short on the slopes, long on the floor

Over the flat floor the cover only has to follow a level surface, so floats are long (typically about 2.75 m) and ballast tubes about 1.83 m. On the side slopes we use short floats and ballast tubes, about 0.91 m each. Short segments let the cover articulate and follow the slope as the water level rises and falls, instead of bridging and straining at the change of grade.

What to put in the specification

  • Cover material, thickness and NSF/ANSI 61 certification where the water is potable.
  • Sump layout drawing, with float and ballast lengths on the floor and on the slopes.
  • Rainwater removal: number and position of sumps and pumps, and the discharge route off the cover.
  • Venting, hatches, walkways and penetrations, fabricated into the panels.
  • Perimeter anchorage detail and the full range of operating water levels.
  • Seam testing to the project CQA plan, and a handover dossier with as-built layout and test records.
